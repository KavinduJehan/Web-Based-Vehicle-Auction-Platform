‌Abstract

When writing an abstract for a software implementation project dissertation, you should provide a concise summary that captures the essence of the entire project. Begin by briefly stating the problem or need that motivated the development of the software, followed by a clear description of the objective or purpose of the system. Next, outline the main technologies, tools, or methodologies used in the implementation, keeping the details high-level. Highlight the key features or functionalities developed, and then summarize the results or outcomes, such as how well the system performed or what feedback was received from users or testing. Conclude the abstract with a statement on the significance or potential impact of the project, including any suggestions for future enhancements. The abstract should typically be written after the project is complete and must fit to a single page.

Keep the tone formal, sincere, and concise, typically within one short paragraph.

‌Chapter 1 Introduction

When writing the Introduction chapter of your software implementation project dissertation, focus on clearly setting the context and purpose of your work. Begin with the motivation, explaining the background of the problem and why the project is important or relevant. This could include real-world challenges and/or personal interest. Then clearly state the aims and objectives of the project: the aim should broadly describe what the project sets out to achieve, while the objectives should be specific, measurable tasks that guide the project’s development towards implementing a complete and effective solution. Finally, define the scope and limitations of the project by outlining what your system will and will not cover. Use formal and clear language, and keep the chapter well-structured to address each of these components explicitly, as they are included in assessment criteria. Typical subsections include:

  • Problem and background;

  • Motivation;

  • Aims, and Objectives of the Project; and

  • Scope of the Project.

When writing the scope of your work, it's important to differentiate it from simply listing features or functional requirements. The scope defines the overall boundaries of the project. It explains what the system will cover, the target users, the environment it will operate in, and the type of problems it is intended to solve. It gives a high-level understanding of what the project includes and excludes. In contrast, a list of features or functional requirements is a detailed, technical breakdown you have to write in the analysis chapter. While the scope may mention major features as part of its explanation, it should focus more on the purpose, coverage, and limitations of the project rather than itemizing every specific function. This distinction helps keep the introduction conceptual and avoids unnecessary detail early in the dissertation.

Further, provide an outline of the thesis starting from chapter 2. These descriptions should be very brief, with one or two sentences for each chapter.

This chapter may be only a few pages in length, but it is one of the most important chapters of the thesis. It will shape the expectations of the reader, and provide them with a guide to the rest of the work.

‌Chapter 2 – Analysis

In the Analysis chapter of your software implementation project, the focus is on thoroughly understanding and defining the problem and system requirements before starting design or development. Begin by describing the current system and clearly outlining existing challenges or inefficiencies. Use flowcharts to illustrate the existing processes and point where issues arise, providing a clear picture of the problem and context.

Next, review similar systems, referencing published studies, software tools, or case examples that have addressed comparable problems. This review supports your design choices by showing how others have successfully approached similar challenges, establishing a foundation for your proposed solution. Following this, clearly identify and prioritize the functional and non-functional requirements of your system. Functional requirements define what the system must do, such as user authentication or data processing, while non-functional requirements cover performance, usability, and reliability.

Importantly, security should be included as a critical functional requirement because modern systems depend on various security controls to operate safely and effectively. These include access controls, data encryption, audit logging, and input validation, all of which are essential functions that protect the system’s integrity, confidentiality, and availability. Treating security as a core functional requirement ensures it is integrated into the system from the beginning rather than as an afterthought.

Finally, justify the choice of your Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC) model by explaining why it best fits your project’s scope and complexity by minimizing the implementation defects and risks. Also, provide reasoning for your selection of programming languages, databases, and other technologies based on their relevance, efficiency, and ability to meet your project’s needs. This chapter demonstrates that your system design is grounded in a clear understanding of the problem, requirements, and practical solutions.

Typical subsections include:


    1. Analysis

      1. Existing System and Problem Description (Describe how the existing system or manual process works. Explain the limitations, errors, bottlenecks, or risks in the current system. Include one or more flowcharts to visually demonstrate the current process and highlight where issues occur.);

      2. Review of Similar Systems (Briefly describe 2–3 similar systems or solutions. Include a feature comparison table.);

      3. System Requirements Analysis; and

        1. Functional Requirements (List and explain the core features your system must provide);

        2. Security as a Functional Requirement (Justify why access control, encryption, input validation, etc., are essential functions for secure system operation.); and

        3. Non-Functional Requirements (Specify quality attributes like performance, reliability, scalability, usability, and maintainability.).

      4. Justification of Development Approach and Technologies.

        1. Chosen SDLC Model and Rationale (Explain which SDLC model (e.g., Waterfall, Iterative, etc.) you selected and why it's suitable for your project in terms of scope, complexity, and risk control.); and

        2. Technology Stack Justification (Justify your choice of programming languages, databases, and tools based on performance, relevance, team expertise, or technical requirements.).

‌Chapter 3 – Design

In the Design chapter of your software implementation project, the objective is to convert the system requirements into a structured and detailed plan for implementation. This involves defining the system architecture, internal module behavior, data models, and user interfaces, with all design elements clearly traceable to the requirements established in the Analysis chapter. Begin by outlining the overall system architecture using diagrams such as layered architecture diagrams or deployment diagrams. These help visualize how the core components—user interface, application logic, database, and external systems—interact within the system.

For internal component design, the approach will depend on whether you are using object-oriented or non-object-oriented design. In object-oriented systems, use case diagrams, class diagrams, sequence diagrams, and activity diagrams should be used to describe user goals, object structures, interactions, and process flows. In non-object-oriented systems, while use case diagrams can still illustrate system functionalities, you should also use data flow diagrams (DFDs), structure charts, and flowcharts to represent the flow of data and control between functional modules. Adapted sequence diagrams may be used to show the temporal interaction between functions or system components, and activity diagrams remain applicable in both paradigms to model workflows and system behavior.

Data modeling is a critical part of the design chapter. Use Entity-Relationship Diagrams (ERDs) to describe the structure of the system’s database, detailing entities, attributes, relationships, and constraints. Discuss normalization, data integrity rules, and key constraints to justify your data model. For user interface design, present wireframes or mockups of key screens and justify your design decisions based on usability, accessibility, and responsiveness. Mention design principles like consistency and simplicity, and any tools used to develop the interface layout.

Throughout the chapter, explain the design methodologies and principles that guide your system architecture and component design—such as modularity, reusability, and separation of concerns. Specify any design patterns or architectural styles employed, like MVC or layered architecture, and explain their suitability to the system's goals. Finally, provide a mapping between each system requirement and the corresponding design

elements, ensuring that the design comprehensively addresses all user and system needs defined earlier.

Suggested subsections for the chapter could include:

  1. Introduction to System Design;

  2. Design Principles and Methodologies;

  3. System Architecture Overview;

  4. Component and Module Design;

  5. Workflow and Behavioral Modeling;

  6. Data Modeling; and

  7. User Interface Design.

‌Chapter 4 – Implementation

In the Implementation chapter of your dissertation, the focus shifts from design plans to the actual development of the system. This chapter should document how the system was built, including the development environment, coding practices, module structure, important algorithms, and version control mechanisms. It should provide enough technical detail to demonstrate how the design was realized in code, ensuring consistency with the design specifications from the previous chapter.

Start by describing the development and deployment environments used throughout the project. Mention the operating system, programming languages, IDEs, libraries, databases, and any other relevant tools or platforms used. This sets the context for reproducibility and technical compatibility.

Next, present the development practices adopted during implementation. Outline how the system was broken into logical modules or components, and briefly describe the responsibilities and interdependencies of each. Discuss your coding standards, including naming conventions, file organization, and formatting rules. Highlight how you ensured readability, maintainability, and documentation through consistent use of inline comments and structured documentation headers. If code linters, formatters, or static analysis tools were used, mention them here.

Highlight the critical code segments that form the core functionality of the system. These may include authentication logic, key algorithm implementations, data validation routines, or system workflows. Provide annotated code snippets (screenshots with black text (or different colors to show syntax) in white background) where appropriate and explain how they fulfill specific design or functional requirements. Make sure to not to include too much code. Collectively, all the code snippets should not exceed two pages. Where performance or efficiency matters, describe the data structures and algorithms you selected, including their rationale (e.g., time complexity, memory efficiency, scalability). You may also mention how security or error-handling logic is implemented within these sections.

Software development often involves iterative updates to the code. Describe your software version management practices, such as using Git for source code control.

Include how you structured your repositories (e.g., branches, commit messages, tags) and whether you used any platforms like GitHub, GitLab, or Bitbucket. If relevant, mention tools or practices for issue tracking, code reviews, or automated testing.

Throughout the chapter, connect your implementation decisions back to the design specifications and system requirements to show traceability and completeness. This chapter should clearly reflect the translation of plans into working code, emphasizing technical depth, adherence to standards, and maintainability.

‌Chapter 5 – Evaluation

In the Evaluation chapter of your software implementation project, your goal is to demonstrate that the system was thoroughly tested, validated, and accepted by its intended users. This chapter should provide clear evidence that the system meets both its technical and user-defined requirements, and that it performs reliably in real-world scenarios.

Begin by describing the testing strategy you followed to verify and validate the system at different levels. Start with unit testing, where individual functions or modules were tested in isolation to confirm they worked as intended. Mention the tools you used for writing and executing these tests, and include representative test cases or screenshots of test outputs. Then explain your approach to integration testing, showing how components were combined and tested together. Describe how you ensured that data flowed correctly between modules, and note any errors or mismatches that were identified and resolved.

System testing followed once all components were integrated. This stage involves evaluating the system as a whole, in conditions that resemble the real usage environment. Provide examples of full-system test scenarios that reflect the key use cases. For each case, explain the inputs, expected outputs, and actual results. Highlight any performance issues, functional errors, or usability concerns found during this process and how they were fixed. Where appropriate, include logs, test reports, or screen captures as proof of test execution.

In addition to technical testing, user feedback is essential. Describe how you conducted User Acceptance Testing (UAT), where actual users or representatives from your target audience interacted with the system. Explain how the testing sessions were organized, what tasks users were asked to perform, and how you gathered feedback (e.g., through interviews, surveys, or observation). Summarize the feedback received, including both positive responses and suggested improvements. Emphasize any design changes or feature adjustments you made in response to this feedback to enhance usability or user satisfaction.

Make sure collectively the space consumed by the screenshots has not exceed 2 pages.

‌Chapter 6 – Conclusion

In the Conclusion chapter of your software implementation project, the objective is to reflect on the overall journey of the project, from problem identification to system delivery, and evaluate its success and impact. This chapter should provide a critical assessment of what was achieved, what challenges were encountered, and how effectively the original goals were met. It should not simply summarize previous chapters, but rather synthesize your findings and insights, demonstrating your understanding of the technical, practical, and personal aspects of the project.

Begin by critically evaluating the effectiveness of your solution. Discuss whether the system met the core functional and non-functional requirements, and how well it addressed the original problem. Consider the design decisions, technologies used, and development process highlighting what worked well and where limitations were encountered. Mention any trade-offs or compromises made during implementation and justify them. This is your opportunity to show that you can assess your own work objectively, identifying strengths and acknowledging weaknesses.

Next, include a personal reflection on the learning experience gained through the project. Reflect on how the work contributed to your understanding of the module’s Intended Learning Outcomes (ILOs), such as applying software development principles, working independently, solving technical problems, and engaging in critical thinking. Mention specific skills you developed or improved, such as time management, use of development tools, communication, or documentation. Be honest and constructive; this is not about listing achievements but about recognizing growth and areas for further development.

Finally, discuss potential future work. Identify limitations in your current system that could be addressed in the next phase or by another developer. Suggest enhancements or new features that could improve functionality, scalability, or user experience. You may also reflect on how emerging technologies or alternative design approaches could benefit the system.

This chapter is your opportunity to wrap up your project with insight and maturity. It should leave the reader with a clear understanding of what was achieved, how it was evaluated, what you learned, and how the work could be extended from where you left off.

    Writing Style


    The following are some of the general points you should use them to improve writing style:


  • Try to minimize mixing of unrelated issues in the same chapter. For example, in the design chapter, do not discuss testing issues. One exception to this rule would be when arguments need to justify actions related to the current topic.

  • Throughout the dissertation, use standard software engineering or computer science terminology. (refer Guide to the Software Engineering Body of Knowledge Version 3.0 SWEBOK®,)

  • Use a consistent naming convention. Do not refer to the same concept or “thing” by different names in different parts of the dissertation.

  • Write short, focused, and coherent paragraphs. Each paragraph should have a clearly stated topic sentence that describes the main issue/context covered in the paragraph. The rest of the sentences should provide discussions on the topic sentence, or answer the question raised in the topic sentence.

  • Do a grammar and style check before submission. It is ESSENTIAL that you proofread your dissertation manually before printing the final copy for submission.

  • Omit needless words—redundant modifiers, pompous diction, excessive detail.


  • Eliminate "narration," expressions such as "It is my opinion that," "I have concluded," "the main point supporting my view concerns," or "certainly there is little doubt as to. ". Focus attention solely on what the reader needs to know.
